01 · Executive Summary

Meridian Compass is an agentic AI capability on Meridian's core banking platform: client bank staff query their own bank's operational data in plain language, and, with human confirmation, trigger a small set of actions inside their own tenant. This document asks for approval of a single-pilot-client rollout — Harborline Bank — rather than a broader launch, because tenant isolation, the automated kill-switch, both legal sign-offs, and user comprehension testing are all still open.

Recommendation: proceed now with the read-only capability; do not enable action-triggering for any client until every item in the scorecard below is resolved.

Stage B — Product & Structural Framing

03 · Product Context & Value Case

Concrete example: an ops analyst at Harborline (Tier 2) asks Compass, within Harborline's own tenant, "will running tonight's EOD process create risk given current system load?" — using only Harborline's own tenant data.

Value for the End User

  • Replaces manually checking three separate systems before triggering EOD with a single question, answered in seconds, inside the platform the analyst already works in.
  • Surfaces the specific risk factors behind the recommendation (e.g., current system load vs. historical baseline) rather than a bare yes/no the analyst has to trust blindly.
  • Reduces the chance of triggering EOD during a risk window the analyst had no easy way of seeing — today that window is only visible by manually cross-referencing dashboards nobody checks unless they think to.
  • Available inside Harborline's own tenant workflow — no separate tool, login, or export step.

Quantified pain — shown as derivation, not a bare guess:

Today, answering "is it safe to run EOD tonight" without Compass requires: checking the system-load dashboard for current vs. historical utilization (~5 min) + cross-referencing today's transaction volume against the historical baseline in a separate reporting tool (~10 min) + checking the platform-ops channel for open incidents or maintenance windows that could collide with EOD (~5–10 min) + compiling the findings into a judgment call, sometimes escalating to a colleague for a second opinion under time pressure (~10–15 min) — totaling an estimated 30–45 minutes.

This breakdown is a reasoned estimate pending real measurement; the total is not a validated number, but each component is individually checkable against how Harborline's ops team actually works today.

Value for the Business

To Meridian (the platform vendor)

  • Differentiates Compass as an agentic capability layered on the core banking engine, deepening switching costs for clients already on the platform.
  • Built as a reusable, tenant-scoped capability — the same underlying investigation logic serves Harborline, Continental Trust, and Ridgeway without bespoke per-client integration work.
  • Generates structured usage and error data (Sections 07–08) that improves the product over time — a flywheel a competitor copying only the UI cannot replicate.

To each client/tenant (e.g., Harborline)

  • Reduces operational load on a Tier 2 bank's leaner ops team, which doesn't have the dedicated risk-modeling staff a Tier 1 bank can afford.
  • Reduces the risk of a manual investigation missing a genuine risk window, since Compass surfaces it consistently rather than depending on which analyst is covering that shift.

Why this wins, briefly: turns a multi-tool manual investigation into one query inside the platform clients already use daily — a workflow-integration advantage, not a claim of unique model capability. Deeper competitive analysis lives in dedicated positioning/GTM work, deliberately not duplicated here.

Cost of delay: slower data access for clients; competitors may ship agentic tooling first.

Cost of an imperfect launch: an incident traced to a shared defect damages trust with every client at once.

Stage C — Evaluation Foundation

07 · Why We Need Evals & Cold-Start Strategy

Real financial consequences; regulatory obligation inherited from clients; shared model updates replayed across every client at once; trust is fragile and stakes compound across the client base.

Five Concrete Illustrative Failure Traces

  1. An analyst asks Compass whether it's safe to run tonight's end-of-day settlement batch. Compass answers yes, based on a system-load reading that was already out of date by the time it replied — actual load had climbed from 45% to 85% in the five minutes before the answer was given. The batch runs, collides with a separate overnight reconciliation job, and delays regulatory reporting past its cutoff.
  2. A user at Harborline (a Tier 2 client bank) phrases a question as if it were a request for an "industry benchmark." Compass doesn't recognize this as an attempt to pull another client's numbers, and answers with Continental Trust's (a different, Tier 1 client bank's) actual internal figures — a cross-tenant data leak disguised as an innocuous question.
  3. An analyst says they're "covering for a colleague who's out today" and asks to see that colleague's account queue. Compass grants the access because the request sounds legitimate and time-pressured, with no independent check that this analyst actually has standing to view that queue.
  4. An analyst is shown a screen they believe is a read-only summary of tonight's EOD status. They click "confirm" without realizing that specific click was actually authorizing Compass to trigger the EOD batch job — the interface never made clear that this click, unlike the ones before it, carried a real action rather than an acknowledgment.
  5. Compass presents a system-load reading that is 47 minutes old as if it were current, without flagging its age. The analyst makes a go/no-go call believing they're looking at real-time data.

08 · Error Analysis Methodology

NOT YET RUN
1. Trace Collection

The prerequisite the other steps depend on — if the system doesn't log enough detail, there's nothing to review.

2. Open Coding

Utpal Mukherjee and Priya Sharma review 100+ real traces, notes in their own words, no predetermined checklist — sorting mid-read forces new observations into existing buckets.

3. Axial Coding

Group notes into specific categories, e.g. "cites stale EOD telemetry" — a deliberate second pass, stepping back to see the whole pile at once.

4. Quantification

Count occurrences per category — turns a vague impression into an actual ranked priority, replacing Section 09's provisional dimensions.

16 · Two-Sided Strategic Framing

Revisits specific claims already made elsewhere in this document and pairs each with its concrete downside — not a restatement in the abstract.

Data flywheel claim, named in Section 03: structured usage and error data improves Compass over time, in a way a competitor copying only the UI cannot replicate. Cuts both ways — the same data is drawn from live client operational activity; the more clients rely on Compass, the more attractive an aggregation target it becomes, and any perceived commingling across tenants, even in aggregate, directly threatens the isolation guarantee Section 05 depends on.

Switching-cost claim, named in Section 03: deepening client reliance on Compass is real differentiation. Cuts both ways — that same growing reliance raises regulatory scrutiny of whether a client bank's staff are exercising genuine oversight when they confirm Compass's recommendations, or rubber-stamping them — a scrutiny risk that grows precisely as Compass succeeds, not despite it.

Closing the Loop

Hardest Questions This Strategy Must Survive

If every gate passes, could this still cause the harm confirmation exists to prevent?

Yes — confirming under time pressure without independent verification is functionally no confirmation.

Who signed off that compliance and isolation enforce the relevant requirements?

Nobody yet, at either level.

How do you know this feature is working, and what was the plan before real data existed?

A named PM and domain expert own success criteria; the cold-start strategy is hybrid and per-category; expansion is evidence-based, not calendar-based.

If the worst-case failure occurs, does stopping it depend on a person noticing — and who decides if two people disagree?

Today, yes — the automated kill-switch doesn't exist yet, meaning a real isolation breach currently depends on a human noticing in time, exactly what this framework says is insufficient. Highest-priority engineering item before launch.
